
# Word types in Pandunia

## Introduction

One of the first things that people notice about Pandunia is that its words don't ever change.
All words in Pandunia have one unchanging form,
which does not change according to number, case, gender, tense, mood or
any of the other inflectional categories known from other languages.

Compare, for example, what happens to the words in the following sentences in Pandunia and English.

1. *mi ame ho.*
   – I love him.
2. *ho ame mi.*
   – He loves me.

In Pandunia, the words simply change places and that's it.
All words stay the same even when their order changes.
In contrast, in English, *I* changes to *me* and *he* changes to *him*
when the subject and object change places,
and the verb *love* changes to *loves* so that it would agree with the new subject.
Changes like those in English are called grammatical inflections,
and languages that use them are called *synthetic languages*
because they synthesize (or combine) many elements of meaning into one word.

Languages that are free from grammatical inflections are known as *analytic languages*,
because they analyze (or divide) the elements of meaning into many separate words.
In analytic languages the word order is very important,
because the grammatical relationships are encoded into the order of words and not into the words themselves.

Pandunia is an analytic language.
The grammar of Pandunia uses **strictly analytic syntax** without grammatical affixes or inflections.
Grammatical relations are signaled only by **rigid word order** without any semantically empty structure words, like articles.
Every word has contentful meaning that refers to something real,
and each word belongs to a **natural meaning category**.

This type of grammar is particularly friendly for beginners,
because all they have to do is to find the right words and to put them in the right order.
It is convenient also for advanced users who can enjoy the clarity of expression.

## Basic grammatical units

Everybody knows at least two essential units of grammar: words and sentences.
In written texts, words are separated by spaces
and sentences end with a full stop.
However, there are also other grammatical units besides words and sentences.

Grammar involves a hierarchy of grammatical units,
which are listed below from the simplest to the most complex.

1. **Morpheme** is the **smallest part of language** with a meaning or function.
2. **Words** are made up of **one or more morphemes**, f.ex. *un-happi-ness*.
3. **Phrases** are made up of **one or more words**, f.ex. *[a very happy person]*.
4. **Clauses** are made up of **one or more phrases**, f.ex. *[(all people) (can see) (a very happy person)]*.
5. **Sentences** are made up of **one or more clauses**, f.ex. *[(You sleep) and (I keep guard)]*.

Morphemes don't have any grammatical function in Pandunia.
Their only job is to help to build the vocabulary of Pandunia.
So the simplest grammatical unit that one usually thinks of in Pandunia is the word.

## Content words and structure words

A word class is a group of words that share similar roles, meanings and word derivation capabilities.
In Pandunia, word classes belong to two superclasses: content words and structure words.
Content words are the words that name things in the real world, like *human*, *house*, and *walking*.
The job of structure words is to bind content words into meaningful phrases.
They have little meaning or only an occasional meaning in the world outside the language.

Content words convey most of information and meaning.
You can't say anything meaningful without them,
but they don't make any sense without grammar, which consists of rules about word order and about the use of structure words.
In Pandunia, many basic sentences are made up of content words only.
Structure words are needed in long and complex sentences.
Content words are like bricks of information and structure words are like the mortar that holds them together.
Structure words are best explained in the grammar,
and content words are explained in the dictionary.


The key to decoding Pandunia is to know the structure words and the clause constructions.
There is a small and fixed number of both of them, about 40 structure words and less than 10 clause constructions.
They form the molds where an endless amount of content words can be inserted in order to say anything that we can think of.


## Word classes

Structure words are categorized into word classes as follows:

1. Pronouns : words that point to people and things.
2. Prepositions : words that relate things and actions into other things and actions
3. Conjunctions : words that join words, phrases and clauses together

Content words are categorized into word classes by their natural meaning as follows:

1. Nouns (thing words): words for things, ideas, places and people.
2. Adjectives (quality words): words for qualities of nouns, such as good, bad, and big.
3. Adverbs (manner words): words that describe degrees of qualities, such as less, more and very.
4. Numerals (quantity words): words for numbers and quantities.
5. Verbs (action words): words for actions and occurrences, such as *to eat* and *to look*.
6. Interjections (reaction words): words that express immediate emotional reactions, like *oh*, *ah*, *yuck* and *boo*

Even though each word has a primary word class role,
it can be reassigned to a different role depending on its position in syntax.
For example, the word
*ame*
('love') is fundamentally a verb but it can function also as noun and adjective.

*mi* ***ame*** *tu.*
– I **love** you. (verb)  
*tu fikre mi su* ***ame.***
– You think about my **love**. (noun)  
*mi kitabe* ***ame*** *letre.*
– I write a **love** letter. (adjective)
